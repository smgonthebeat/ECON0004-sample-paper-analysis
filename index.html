Part A (40% of Total Mark)

Question A1:

Context: Consider the following model:

$$ln(wage_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 education_i + \beta_2 education_i^2 + \beta_3 white_i + \beta_4 gender_i + \beta_5 white_i \times gender_i + \beta_6 couple_i + \mu_i \quad (1)$$

Where $lnwage_i$ is the natural logarithm of the hourly wage of individual i; $education_i$ is years of formal schooling of individual i; $white_i$ is a dummy variable which takes the value 1 if the individual is white and 0 if black; $gender_i$ is a gender dummy variable taking the value 1 if male and 0 if female; $couple_i$ is a dummy variable taking the value 1 if individual i lives as part of a couple (either married or cohabiting); $\mu_i$ is the error term.

Using the data on a representative sample of workers in the UK (Sample size = 10,000), a researcher runs OLS regression and obtains the following estimates ($R^2 = 0.39$):

Coefficient Point estimate Standard error
$\beta_0$ -0.57 0.003
$\beta_1$ 0.006 0.002
$\beta_2$ -0.045 0.014
$\beta_3$ 0.056 0.028
$\beta_4$ 0.034 0.063
$\beta_5$ 0.023 0.012
$\beta_6$ 0.072 0.0088
(a) What is the return to an additional year of formal schooling? Can we reject that the returns to education are constant? When is the marginal return to education equal to zero?

Answer:

  • Return to another year of schooling: $\frac{\partial ln(wage)}{\partial education} = \beta_1 + 2\beta_2 education$.
  • To test if returns are constant, test $H_0: \beta_2 = 0$ vs $H_1: \beta_2 \neq 0$.
  • $t = \frac{|\hat{\beta}_2|}{se(\hat{\beta}_2)} = \frac{|-0.045|}{0.014} = 3.21$. Since $3.21 > 1.96$ (critical value for 5% significance), reject $H_0$. Returns are not constant.
  • Marginal return is zero when $\hat{\beta}_1 + 2\hat{\beta}_2 education = 0 \implies 0.006 - 2(0.045) education = 0 \implies education = \frac{0.006}{0.09} \approx 0.0667$ years. (Note: The solution mentions this value seems small due to a potential typo in the question estimates).
(b) What does the $R^2$ measure in the regression? Give the formula and explain what the $R^2$ is in words.

Answer:

  • $R^2$ measures the proportion of the sample variation in the dependent variable (log wage) that is explained by the independent variables in the model.
  • Formula: $R^2 = 1 - \frac{RSS}{TSS} = \frac{ESS}{TSS}$, where RSS is Residual Sum of Squares, TSS is Total Sum of Squares, and ESS is Explained Sum of Squares.
  • In this case, $R^2 = 0.39$, meaning 39% of the variation in log wages in the sample is explained by the model.
(c) Holding other variables constant, what is the estimated difference in log hourly wages between white females and non-white females? Is this estimated difference statistically significant?

Answer:

  • For females, $gender = 0$. The difference is $E[ln(wage)|white=1, female] - E[ln(wage)|white=0, female] = \beta_3$.
  • The estimated difference is $\hat{\beta}_3 = 0.056$.
  • Test $H_0: \beta_3 = 0$ vs $H_1: \beta_3 \neq 0$.
  • $t = \frac{|\hat{\beta}_3|}{se(\hat{\beta}_3)} = \frac{0.056}{0.028} = 2$. Since $2 > 1.96$, reject $H_0$. The difference is statistically significant at the 5% level.
(d) Holding other variables constant, what is the estimated difference in log hourly wages between white males and non-white males?

Answer:

  • For males, $gender = 1$. The difference is $E[ln(wage)|white=1, male] - E[ln(wage)|white=0, male]$.
  • $E[ln(wage)|white=1, male] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + \beta_2 educ^2 + \beta_3 + \beta_4 + \beta_5 + \beta_6 couple$
  • $E[ln(wage)|white=0, male] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + \beta_2 educ^2 + \beta_4 + \beta_6 couple$
  • Difference = $\beta_3 + \beta_5$.
  • The estimated difference is $\hat{\beta}_3 + \hat{\beta}_5 = 0.056 + 0.023 = 0.079$.
(e) Construct a test of the hypothesis that being in a couple raises wages by 10%. Can you reject the hypothesis that being in a couple is associated with 10% higher wages?

Answer:

  • A 10% increase corresponds to approximately a 0.1 increase in log wages (log-level interpretation). Test $H_0: \beta_6 = 0.1$ vs $H_1: \beta_6 \neq 0.1$.
  • $t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_6 - 0.1}{se(\hat{\beta}_6)} = \frac{0.072 - 0.1}{0.0088} = \frac{-0.028}{0.0088} \approx -3.18$.
  • Since $|-3.18| > 1.96$, reject $H_0$. We can reject the hypothesis that being in a couple is associated with 10% higher wages.
(f) Do you see any problems with the empirical model in equation (1), as an estimate of the financial returns to education?

Answer: (Sketch answer points)

  • Omitted Variable Bias (OVB): Key variables like 'ability' might be omitted. Ability is likely positively correlated with both education and wages, leading to an upward bias in the estimated return to education. Violation of $E[\epsilon|X]=0$.
  • Functional Form: The quadratic form might not be correct; returns might interact with gender or other variables.
  • Measurement Error: 'Years of schooling' might be a poor measure of educational attainment compared to specific qualifications.

Part B (Answer TWO Questions - 30% Each)

Question B1: Demand System

Context: A researcher working for a chain of gastro-pubs, estimates the following demand system for food prepared and eaten at home (food at home), and purchases of food prepared outside the home (restaurant and takeaway meals; food out), for a sample of 2500 working-age urban households observed during 2015:

Variable $w_{fout}$ Coef. (t-value) $w_{fhome}$ Coef. (t-value)
$lnY$ 0.062 (4.22) -0.133 (4.67)
$lnP_{fout}$ -0.042 (3.13) 0.008 (2.44)
$lnP_{fhome}$ 0.012 (2.01) 0.020 (3.21)
People 0.002 (0.46) 0.038 (4.33)
Head FT 0.016 (2.59) -0.003 (2.06)
Constant 0.019 (0.98) 0.160 (4.22)
$R^2$ 0.26 0.45
Mean, dependent variable 0.065 0.196

Where: $w_{fout}$ is the budget share on food prepared outside the home; $w_{fhome}$ is the budget share on food prepared at home; $lnY$ is the log of household expenditure; $lnP_{fout}$ is the log of the price of food out; $lnP_{fhome}$ is the log of the price of food at home; People is the number of people in the household; Head FT is a dummy variable taking the value 1 if the head of household works a full time, and 0 otherwise; t-values are reported in absolute terms.

(a) What is the interpretation of the coefficient on People in each equation? Can we reject the hypothesis that the number of people in the household does not affect spending on food out? Can we reject the same hypothesis for spending on food at home?

Answer:

  • Coefficient on People: An extra person increases the budget share on food out by 0.002 and food at home by 0.038, holding other factors constant.
  • Food out: $H_0: \beta_{People} = 0$. The t-value is 0.46. Since $|0.46| < 1.96$, we cannot reject $H_0$. No significant effect on food out share.
  • Food home: $H_0: \beta_{People} = 0$. The t-value is 4.33. Since $|4.33| > 1.96$, we reject $H_0$. Significant positive effect on food at home share.
(b) Consider a household with an average budget share on each good. Can we say about whether food in and food out seem to be luxuries or necessities?

Answer:

  • Use the formula for income elasticity in a budget share model: $\xi_{w,y} = 1 + \frac{\beta_{lnY}}{w}$. Average shares are $w_{fout} = 0.065$ and $w_{fhome} = 0.196$.
  • Food out: $\xi_{fout,Y} = 1 + \frac{0.062}{0.065} \approx 1.954$. Since $\xi_{fout,Y} > 1$ (or because $\beta_{lnY} = 0.062 > 0$), food out appears to be a luxury. The coefficient is statistically significant (t=4.22).
  • Food home: $\xi_{fhome,Y} = 1 + \frac{-0.133}{0.196} \approx 1 - 0.679 = 0.321$. Since $0 < \xi_{fhome,Y} < 1$ (or because $\beta_{lnY} = -0.133 < 0$), food at home appears to be a necessity. The coefficient is statistically significant (t=4.67).
(c) Do the reported own price terms suggest the demand for food in and food out are price elastic, or price inelastic? Are the cross price terms consistent with economic theory?

Answer:

  • Own-price elasticity interpretation in this model depends on the coefficient $\gamma_{jj}$ on $lnP_j$.
  • Food out: Coefficient on $lnP_{fout}$ is $\gamma_{fout,fout} = -0.042$ (significant, t=3.13). A negative coefficient suggests elastic demand in this context.
  • Food home: Coefficient on $lnP_{fhome}$ is $\gamma_{fhome,fhome} = 0.020$ (significant, t=3.21). A positive coefficient suggests inelastic demand in this context.
  • Cross-price terms: Coefficient of $lnP_{fhome}$ in the $w_{fout}$ equation ($\gamma_{fout,fhome}$) is 0.012 (positive, significant). Coefficient of $lnP_{fout}$ in the $w_{fhome}$ equation ($\gamma_{fhome,fout}$) is 0.008 (positive, significant). Positive cross-price coefficients suggest the goods are substitutes, which is consistent with economic theory.
(d) What are the advantages of using a budget-share model such as this in contrast to a double-log specification for spending on each good? Do you think these are relevant in the context of spending on food?

Answer:

  • Advantages: Budget share models can handle zero expenditures (double-log requires positive values); they allow for non-constant elasticities (double-log implies constant elasticity $\beta_1$).
  • Relevance: Zero expenditures might occur for 'food out', making the budget share model suitable. Elasticities for food items are unlikely to be constant across all income/price levels.
(e) In a separate estimation model based only on rural households, the researcher finds that the coefficient on lnY in the food out equation is 0.028. Can we reject the hypothesis that the equivalent parameter reported in the table has the value 0.028? Do you think it is plausible that the effect of total budget (lnY) or of prices on demand for food out, might be different for rural and urban households? Explain your answer.

Answer:

  • Test $H_0: \beta_{lnY, fout} = 0.028$ vs $H_1: \beta_{lnY, fout} \neq 0.028$. The original estimate is $\hat{\beta}_{lnY, fout} = 0.062$ with t=4.22.
  • First, find the standard error: $se(\hat{\beta}_{lnY, fout}) = \frac{|\hat{\beta}_{lnY, fout}|}{|t|} = \frac{0.062}{4.22} \approx 0.0147$.
  • $t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_{lnY, fout} - 0.028}{se(\hat{\beta}_{lnY, fout})} = \frac{0.062 - 0.028}{0.0147} = \frac{0.034}{0.0147} \approx 2.31$. Since $|2.31| > 1.96$, reject $H_0$. The coefficient is significantly different from 0.028.
  • Plausibility: Yes, it's plausible. Rural and urban households might differ in availability of restaurants ('food out'), income levels, lifestyles, and prices, leading to different demand responses.

Question B2: Difference-in-Differences

Context: Suppose in 2014 the government introduces a new policy targeted at decreasing unemployment amongst disadvantaged groups in designated areas of high unemployment. One part of this policy is an employment rehabilitation policy that provides a subsidy to employers for every worker they employ with a criminal record. Data are collected on the unemployment rates of a group of workers without criminal records (group A) and a group of workers with criminal records (group B) before and after the policy is introduced.

2013 (before) 2015 (after)
Group A: No criminal records 23.25% 20.37%
Group B: criminal records 49.46% 42.86%
(a) Using the table above, compare the unemployment rate of each group in 2013. Do the data reflect a causal effect of having a criminal record on the unemployment rate?

Answer:

  • Comparison 2013: Group B (criminal record) unemployment = 49.46%, Group A (no record) = 23.25%. Difference = 49.46% - 23.25% = 26.21%.
  • Causality: Unlikely to be purely causal. Potential issues include: omitted variables (e.g., education, ability correlated with both criminal records and unemployment), reverse causality (unemployment might lead to crime), selection bias. Correlation does not imply causation.
(b) Using the data in the table above, calculate an estimate of the effect of the policy on the unemployment rate. Be clear about how you would calculate it. Illustrate the difference-in-differences estimator of the effect of the policy graphically.

Answer:

  • DiD Calculation: $(\overline{y}_{Treat,After} - \overline{y}_{Treat,Before}) - (\overline{y}_{Control,After} - \overline{y}_{Control,Before})$.
  • Change for Treatment (Group B): $42.86\% - 49.46\% = -6.60\%$.
  • Change for Control (Group A): $20.37\% - 23.25\% = -2.88\%$.
  • DiD Estimate = $(-6.60\%) - (-2.88\%) = -3.72\%$.
  • Interpretation: The policy is estimated to have reduced the unemployment rate for those with criminal records by 3.72 percentage points, relative to the change experienced by those without criminal records.
  • Graphical Illustration: A graph showing two lines (one for Group A, one for Group B) plotting unemployment rates in 2013 and 2015. The DiD estimate is the vertical difference between the actual 2015 point for Group B and the point where Group B's line would have ended if it followed the same trend as Group A's line (parallel trend assumption). (Note: Graph not rendered here, but described).
(c) Carefully explain the assumptions required for the difference-in-differences estimator to be valid. Are these reasonable in this case?

Answer:

  • Key Assumption: Parallel Trends. In the absence of the treatment (policy), the average change in the outcome variable (unemployment rate) would have been the same for both the treatment and control groups.
  • Other related assumptions: No spillover/general equilibrium effects, stable group composition/no selection effects related to treatment.
  • Reasonableness:
    • Parallel Trends/No GE Effects: Questionable. Hiring subsidized workers (Group B) might displace non-subsidized workers (potentially affecting Group A if they compete for similar jobs). Also, the groups might work in different sectors experiencing different economic trends.
    • Composition Effects: Probably less problematic.
(d) In order to evaluate this policy, you run the following regression: $Unemployment_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 After_i + \beta_2 Treat_i + \beta_3 (After_i \times Treat_i) + \epsilon_i$. Which regression coefficient will measure the difference-in-differences estimate of the policy? Show the derivation and explain your answer.

Context Note: Variable definitions provided: $Unemployment_i$ is a dummy variable equal to 1 if the observation i is unemployed and 0 otherwise; $After_i$ is a dummy variable that takes the value 1 if the observation i is in year 2015 (after the policy is introduced) and 0 otherwise; $Treat_i$ is a dummy variable that takes the value 1 if the observation i is from treatment group and 0 otherwise; $After_i \times Treat_i$ is the interaction between the two variables; $\epsilon_i$ is the error term.

Answer:

  • The coefficient $\beta_3$ measures the DiD estimate.
  • Derivation:
    • $E[Unemp | After=0, Treat=0] = \beta_0$
    • $E[Unemp | After=1, Treat=0] = \beta_0 + \beta_1$
    • $E[Unemp | After=0, Treat=1] = \beta_0 + \beta_2$
    • $E[Unemp | After=1, Treat=1] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3$
  • Calculate DiD:
    • Change in Treatment: $E[After=1, Treat=1] - E[After=0, Treat=1] = (\beta_0 + \beta_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3) - (\beta_0 + \beta_2) = \beta_1 + \beta_3$
    • Change in Control: $E[After=1, Treat=0] - E[After=0, Treat=0] = (\beta_0 + \beta_1) - \beta_0 = \beta_1$
    • DiD = (Change in Treatment) - (Change in Control) = $(\beta_1 + \beta_3) - \beta_1 = \beta_3$.
(e) What are the advantages of implementing the difference-in-differences estimator using a regression, rather than the simpler method described in part (b)?

Answer:

  • Provides standard errors for the DiD estimate ($\hat{\beta}_3$), allowing for formal hypothesis testing on the policy effect's significance.
  • Allows inclusion of additional control variables (covariates) to potentially improve the precision of the estimate and control for compositional differences between groups.

Question B3: Labour Supply

Context: Consider the following equation, which shows estimates from a model of the labour supply of 4000 married men in semi-skilled occupations in London and the South West.

$$Hours = \underset{(6.12)}{28.98} - \underset{(0.07)}{0.14} Y + \underset{(0.24)}{9.6} W - \underset{(0.06)}{0.12} W^2 - \underset{(6.54)}{7.82} K + \underset{(2.35)}{5.28} Health$$

where numbers in brackets are standard errors and the following table gives details of the regression variables:

Variable Name Description
Hours Hours worked per week
Y Wife's income (pounds per week)
W Wage (pounds per hour)
K Dummy variable = 1 if he has children, 0 otherwise
Health Dummy variable = 1 if he is healthy, 0 otherwise
(a) According to economic theory, what happens to hours of work when wage increases? Draw graphs to illustrate your answer.

Answer:

  • A wage increase has two opposing effects:
    • Substitution Effect: Higher wage increases the opportunity cost of leisure, encouraging more work.
    • Income Effect: Higher wage increases purchasing power. If leisure is a normal good, this encourages more leisure (less work).
  • The net effect depends on which effect dominates. The labour supply curve can be upward sloping (substitution > income) or backward bending (income > substitution at higher wage levels).
  • Graphs typically show indifference curves between consumption and leisure, budget constraints shifting with wage changes, and the resulting labour supply curve. (Note: Graph not rendered here, but described).
(b) Is there evidence of backward bending labour supply in this model? If so, at what point does it occur?

Answer:

  • Yes, there is evidence. The coefficient on $W$ is positive (9.6) and significant ($t = 9.6/0.24 = 40 > 1.64$). The coefficient on $W^2$ is negative (-0.12) and significant ($t = -0.12/0.06 = -2 < -1.64$). This quadratic form indicates a curve that increases and then decreases.
  • The turning point occurs where the marginal effect of wage on hours is zero: $\frac{\partial Hours}{\partial W} = 9.6 - 2(0.12)W = 0$.
  • $9.6 = 0.24W \implies W = \frac{9.6}{0.24} = 40$. The labour supply curve bends backward at a wage of £40 per hour.
(c) Holding other variables constant, what is the estimated difference in weekly working hours between men with children and men without children? Is this estimated difference statistically significant?

Answer:

  • The difference is given by the coefficient on $K$ (Dummy = 1 if children), which is -7.82 hours per week. Men with children are estimated to work 7.82 hours less per week, ceteris paribus.
  • Test $H_0: \beta_K = 0$ vs $H_1: \beta_K \neq 0$.
  • $t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_K}{se(\hat{\beta}_K)} = \frac{-7.82}{6.54} \approx -1.196$. Since $|-1.196| < 1.96$, we cannot reject $H_0$ at the 5% level. The estimated difference is not statistically significant.
(d) Show that the elasticity of labour supply with respect to the wage depends on health status in this empirical model.

Answer:

  • Elasticity formula: $\epsilon_{H,W} = \frac{\partial Hours}{\partial W} \times \frac{W}{Hours}$.
  • $\frac{\partial Hours}{\partial W} = 9.6 - 0.24W$.
  • $Hours = 28.98 - 0.14 Y + 9.6 W - 0.12 W^2 - 7.82 K + 5.28 Health$.
  • Substitute these into the elasticity formula: $$\epsilon_{H,W} = (9.6 - 0.24W) \times \frac{W}{28.98 - 0.14 Y + 9.6 W - 0.12 W^2 - 7.82 K + 5.28 Health}$$
  • Since the denominator ($Hours$) explicitly includes the $Health$ term, the overall elasticity $\epsilon_{H,W}$ depends on the value of the $Health$ dummy variable.
(e) Note that the data for this study is a sample of workers only. What is the problem with using these estimates to make statements about the preferences for work of individuals currently unemployed?

Answer:

  • Sample Selection Bias: The sample only includes employed individuals. Unemployed individuals might have systematically different characteristics or preferences for work compared to those employed. Using estimates from a selected sample (workers only) to infer the behaviour of the entire population (including non-workers) can lead to biased conclusions.

To succeed in this exam, a solid grasp of the following is needed:

  • OLS Regression: Multiple regression principles, assumptions (especially exogeneity $E[\epsilon|X]=0$), estimation, and interpretation.
  • Functional Forms: Facility with linear, log-level, level-log, log-log, quadratic forms, budget shares, dummy variables, and interactions, understanding their implications for interpretation (e.g., marginal effects, elasticities).
  • Hypothesis Testing: Conducting and interpreting t-tests for significance and specific values, using t-statistics ($t = (\hat{\beta}_j - \beta_{j,0}) / se(\hat{\beta}_j)$) and critical values/p-values.
  • Goodness-of-Fit: Understanding and interpreting $R^2$ ($1 - RSS/TSS$).
  • Causality vs. Correlation: Recognizing potential biases like Omitted Variable Bias (OVB) and Sample Selection Bias, and familiarity with techniques like Difference-in-Differences (DiD) for causal inference.
  • Economic Application: Applying these econometric tools within specific economic contexts (labour supply, consumer demand) and linking results to economic theory.
  • Data Interpretation: Reading and extracting information from regression output tables (coefficients, standard errors, $t$-values, $R^2$).

The exam tests both technical econometric skills and the ability to interpret results meaningfully in an economic context.
